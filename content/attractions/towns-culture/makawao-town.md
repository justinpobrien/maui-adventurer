Makawao sits about 1,600 feet up the slopes of Haleakala, and its identity is built on a genuinely distinctive history: Hawaii's paniolo (cowboy) tradition traces back to 1793, when the first cattle arrived from California as a gift to King Kamehameha I, and to 1830, when Mexican vaqueros were brought over to teach Hawaiians how to herd them. By the early 1900s, Makawao was home to more than 60 cattle ranches, and that heritage is still visible and celebrated today.

The town's early 20th-century wooden buildings now house an eclectic mix of boutiques, a yoga studio, and cafes, alongside standout cultural stops: Komoda Store & Bakery (Maui's oldest bakery, open since 1916, famous for cream puffs and malasadas), Viewpoints Gallery (Maui's only fine arts cooperative), Maui Hands (featuring work from over 300 local artists), and the Hui No'eau Visual Arts Center, set in a historic mansion on Baldwin Avenue.

The paniolo heritage comes to life most vividly every Fourth of July weekend during the Makawao Rodeo, a genuine working-cowboy competition with barrel racing, cattle wrangling, bareback bronco riding, and calf roping — a distinctly different cultural experience than anything found on Maui's coast.
